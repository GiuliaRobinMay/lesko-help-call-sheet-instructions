How to build a call sheet that actually gets you money

A call sheet is a list of the offices and organizations near you that hand out grants and financial help — with the phone number to reach each one. It is the single most important tool in this community. This page shows you how to get a good one, and what to do with it.

What a call sheet is

A call sheet is a list of addresses and phone numbers of places you can contact to find grants and financial help. It is always built from two things:

  • Where you live — your ZIP code and your city and state. Almost all help is local. A program in the next county over may not be able to touch your case.
  • What you need — the specific problem you want money for.

Put those two things together and you get a page of real organizations, with real phone numbers, that have money for people in your situation. Then you pick up the phone.

Why this matters more than searching online

Most of this money is never advertised. It sits with a county office, a community action agency, a hospital foundation, a utility hardship fund, a church coalition — places that do not run ads and do not appear on page one of a search engine. They give it out to the people who call and ask.

A call sheet turns "I don't know where to start" into a list of twenty-five phone numbers. That is the whole trick.

The golden rule

One problem, one call sheet

Never combine several problems into one list. Make a separate call sheet for each problem.

It is tempting to ask for everything at once — rent, the car, the medical bills, the business idea. Don't. A combined list gives you twenty-five organizations that are each a weak match for something. Four separate lists give you a hundred organizations that are each a strong match for one thing.

Do this

  • Sheet 1 — Help paying rent and utilities in Toledo, OH
  • Sheet 2 — Car repair assistance in Toledo, OH
  • Sheet 3 — Startup funding for a home bakery in Toledo, OH

Not this

  • One sheet for "rent, my car, my medical bills and my business idea in Toledo, OH"

You will get a shallow, generic list — and you will not know which number to call about what.

Have more than one problem? Good. Make more than one call sheet. There is no limit, and each one takes minutes to request.

    Still not sure what to ask for?

    Ask yourself these three questions. The answer to any one of them is a good call sheet.

    • What bill is keeping you up at night? That is your first sheet.
    • What would change your life in six months? A working car, a certification, a business licence. That is your second sheet.
    • What do you keep saying no to because you can't afford it? Dental work, a repair, a move. That is your third sheet.

    Bring the answer to a class exactly as you wrote it. Our grant coaches would much rather refine a specific request than guess at a vague one.

    Step 2

    Three ways to get your call sheet

    These are not alternatives to choose between. Try all three. Each one finds organizations the others miss, and members who use all three end up with the deepest lists.

    Way one

    Join a Call Sheet Class

    A live class where the team walks you through what a call sheet is, how it is built, and how to use it once you have it. You can watch it being done rather than reading about it.

    If there is time after the class, team members will build call sheets for members live. They will take down your problem, your ZIP code and your state — so have all three ready before you join.

    Best when: you are new, or something about your situation is complicated and you want a human being to look at it.

    Way two

    Use the AI Researcher app

    Our own AI tool, built for this community. Enter your ZIP code and your problem, and it builds your list and gives it to you as a spreadsheet you can download.

    It is the fastest of the three — you can have a sheet in minutes, at 2am, without waiting for anyone. You can also run it again and again with different wording and compare what comes back.

    Best when: you want a list right now, or you want several lists for several problems.

    Way three

    Ask in the Questions Channel

    Post your request and a team member will build a call sheet for you and post it back, usually as a shareable spreadsheet with notes about your specific situation.

    You can ask more than one question, and you can ask for more than one sheet — just make each request a separate post for a separate problem.

    Best when: you want a human-checked list and you are happy to wait for a reply. Also the right place for follow-up questions.

    Do all three, in this order

    1. Run the AI Researcher first. It costs you nothing but a few minutes and gives you a list to react to. Reacting to a list is far easier than starting from a blank page.
    2. Bring that list to a Call Sheet Class or an AI Workshop. Now you have something specific to ask about: "number 14 has no phone number", "I don't understand what number 8 means". This is the fastest way to learn.
    3. Post the leftovers in the Questions Channel. Anything still unclear, and anything the AI could not find, goes there for a team member to dig into.

    Nervous about showing up? Come anyway.

    Please do not let discomfort keep you from asking for help. Every person in that room came because they needed something, and nobody is judging you.

    And if you would rather not be seen or recognised, that is completely fine — use an avatar picture instead of a photo, and a different display name. Turn your camera off. Type your question in the chat instead of speaking it. You will get exactly the same help.

    The only thing that stops you getting a call sheet is not asking for one.

    What a complete call sheet contains

    This is the column set our team uses. If your sheet is missing these, it is not finished — ask for the missing pieces.

    # Column Why you need it
    01Name of organizationWho you are calling. Say it back to them when they answer.
    02WebsiteThe full link. Your first stop for checking the sheet is right.
    03Email addressYour backup when nobody picks up the phone.
    04Phone numberWith the direct extension if there is one — it skips the switchboard.
    05Contact personA name to ask for gets you past the front desk.
    06Eligibility requirementsWhether you qualify. Read this before dialling so you don't waste a call.
    07Area requirementsWhich counties or ZIP codes they serve. This is why your ZIP matters.
    08Dates of eligibilityApplication windows and deadlines. Some open once a year.
    09Services availableWhat they actually do, in plain words.
    10Dollar value of free servicesFree help has a price tag. Counselling, training and legal aid are worth real money.
    11Grants or loans availableMoney you keep versus money you repay. Know which you are applying for.
    12Maximum amountLets you sort the list and call the biggest ones first.
    13Things to consider before applyingThe catches — restrictions, waiting lists, documents you'll need.
    1430-second call scriptExactly what to say when they answer. Removes the hardest part of the call.

    Two habits that separate good sheets from great ones

    • Ask for 25 organizations, not 10. A realistic response rate means most calls end in "no" or "not right now". Twenty-five names is roughly what it takes to find the two or three that say yes.
    • Ask for a mix of sources. Government offices, non-profits, private foundations, corporate giving programs and niche organizations for your specific situation. A sheet that is all government agencies is missing half the money.
    Step 3

    Check the sheet before you call

    Ten minutes of checking saves you an afternoon of dead numbers.

    AI can make mistakes — and phone numbers are where it makes them most

    AI tools are extremely good at finding organizations you would never have heard of. They are much less reliable at getting the contact details exactly right. Independent testing has found that roughly a third of phone numbers produced by AI assistants do not match the organization's own published number.

    That does not make the tool useless — it makes verification part of the job. A wrong number is a two-minute fix, not a reason to throw the list away.

    Your ten-minute check

    • Open the website column first. If the link is dead or goes somewhere unrelated, treat everything in that row as unverified.
    • Compare the phone number to the one on the organization's own contact page. If they differ, the website's number is the right one.
    • Check the area column against your ZIP code. Programs that don't serve your county cannot help you, no matter how good they sound.
    • Check the dates. A program whose window closed last month goes to the bottom of the list, with a note to call back.
    • Mark anything you couldn't confirm. Don't delete it — flag it and bring it to a class.

    Found something wrong? Bring it to a class.

    If a name looks off, a phone number doesn't work, or a listing simply doesn't make sense — do not just cross it out and move on. Join a Call Sheet Class, an AI Workshop or a Q&A and ask a grant coach about it.

    Nine times out of ten there is a real organization behind a garbled listing, and a coach can find the correct details in seconds. That is exactly what the classes are for, and it is never a silly question.

    Step 4

    Work the list

    A call sheet sitting in your downloads folder has never paid anybody's rent.

    Before you start dialling

    • Gather your paperwork. Copies of the bills you need help with, proof of income, proof of address, ID. Most programs verify these during the application, and having them within reach turns a callback into a same-call decision.
    • Read the call script in column 14 out loud once. The first call is the hardest. The tenth is easy.
    • Block out time. Most of these offices keep normal business hours and many close for lunch. Two hours on a Tuesday morning beats ten minutes here and there.

    On every call, ask these four questions

    1. "Do I qualify for this, based on where I live and my situation?" Get a yes or no before anything else.
    2. "What exactly do I need to send you, and by when?" Write down every document and every deadline.
    3. "Is there anyone else you'd recommend I call?" This is the highest-value question on the whole sheet. People who work at these organizations know every other program in the county. One call routinely produces three more names that were on nobody's list.
    4. "Can I have your name and a direct number?" A named contact makes every follow-up call ten times easier.

    Write down the outcome of every single call

    Add columns to your sheet and fill them in as you go. This record becomes as valuable as the original list — it stops you calling twice, and it is what you bring to a class when you want help figuring out the next move.

    Date called Who I spoke to Outcome Next step Follow up on

    Expect "no" — it is part of the process, not a verdict on you

    Funds run out. Windows close. Criteria shift. A "no" from one organization tells you nothing about the next one, and a program that has no money in August may have a new budget in October. Ask when to call back, note it down, and move to the next line.

    Step 5

    Do it again. And again.

    This is the part that works, and it is the part most people quit before reaching.

    One call sheet is a start. It is not the method. The members who get funded are the ones who repeat this loop over and over:

    • Repeat across your problems. A separate sheet for every distinct need. Four problems means four sheets.
    • Repeat with different wording. Run the AI Researcher again asking for "emergency financial assistance", then "hardship funds", then "utility assistance programs". Different words surface different organizations. This costs you nothing.
    • Repeat across the classes. Attend more than one. Every coach has a different angle, and the questions other members ask will find money you didn't know to look for.
    • Repeat your calls. Every name that said "not right now" goes on a callback list with a date. New budget cycles open constantly.
    • Repeat every few months. Programs launch, close and change all year. A sheet built in spring is out of date by autumn — build a fresh one.

    There is no cap on how many call sheets you can request, how many classes you can attend, or how many questions you can post. Use all of it.

    Reference

    Protecting yourself

    Never post personal details in a class or in the Questions Channel

    Both are visible to other members. Whatever you post, assume a stranger will read it.

    • Your phone number
    • Your email address
    • Your street address
    • Your Social Security number
    • Bank account or card details
    • Your date of birth
    • Photos of any document or ID
    • Case, claim or benefit numbers

    What is safe to share: your city, your state, your ZIP code, and a description of your problem. That is genuinely all anyone needs to build you a good call sheet.

    Being as precise as possible without being identifiable

    Precision helps you. Personal details don't. "A disabled veteran in ZIP 43604 needing roughly $2,400 for back rent" is precise, useful, and identifies nobody. Every organization on your sheet will ask for the private details later — on the phone, directly, where they belong.

    Use an avatar if you'd rather not be recognised

    You are allowed to protect your privacy and still get help. Set a picture that isn't your face and a display name that isn't your legal name. Keep your camera off in classes. None of this reduces the support you receive, and nobody will ask you why.

    Know the difference between a real program and a scam

    Everything on a proper call sheet is an organization you contact. That is the safe direction. Be suspicious of anything that contacts you first:

    • Anyone charging a fee to apply for a grant. Government grant applications are free.
    • Anyone calling to say you have won a grant you never applied for. Government agencies do not do this.
    • Anyone asking for payment by gift card, wire transfer, cash reload card or cryptocurrency. No legitimate program is paid this way.
    • Anyone phoning, texting or messaging you out of the blue for your Social Security number, bank details or a photo of your ID.

    If something feels wrong, stop and bring it to a class before you act on it. You can report grant scams to the Federal Trade Commission at 1-877-382-4357.

    Reference

    Bookmark every question you ask

    When you post in the Questions Channel, bookmark your own post straight away. Replies can arrive days later, and the channel moves fast — without a bookmark you will lose track of where your answer went.

    Your bookmarks live under your personal settings, in Bookmarked Posts. Everything you have saved is in one place, so your call sheets, the coach's notes and your follow-up questions stay together.

    • Bookmark the post the moment you publish it, before you close the tab.
    • Bookmark the reply containing your finished call sheet too — that is the link you will keep coming back to.
    • Bookmark other members' questions when the answer is useful to you. Somebody has usually already asked your question.

    There is fuller guidance on writing a good question pinned inside the Questions Channel itself. Read it once before your first post — it will save you a round trip.

    A note about AI, and about asking for help

    AI can make mistakes. The AI Researcher and every other AI tool can misspell an organization's name, produce a phone number that rings nowhere, list a program that closed last year, or state an eligibility rule slightly wrong. Always check a listing against the organization's own website before you rely on it.

    If anything is unclear or looks wrong, come and ask. Join a Call Sheet Class, an AI Workshop, a Q&A or a drop-in clinic and put it in front of a grant coach. Bring the sheet, point at the row, and ask. Getting the correct details lined up is exactly what our coaches are here for, and there is no such thing as a question that is too small.

    Nothing on this page is legal or financial advice, and no organization listed on any call sheet is guaranteed to have funds available for you.
