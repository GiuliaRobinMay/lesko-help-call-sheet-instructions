01 · What it is The foundation
The basics

A list of places to phone.

A call sheet is a list of addresses and phone numbers of organizations you can contact to find grants and financial help. It is built from two things, and only two things.

01

Where you live

Your ZIP code, your city and your state. Almost all of this money is local — a program in the next county cannot help you, no matter how good it sounds.

02

What you need

The one specific problem you want money for. Not your whole life — one problem.

Why this beats searching online

Most of this money is never advertised. It sits with a county office, a community action agency, a hospital foundation, a utility hardship fund, a church coalition — places that run no ads and never appear on page one of a search engine. They give it out to the people who call and ask.

A call sheet turns "I don't know where to start" into twenty-five phone numbers. That is the whole trick.

02 · The golden rule Never combine
The one rule

One problem. One call sheet.

It is tempting to ask for everything at once — the rent, the car, the medical bills, the business idea. Don't.

A combined list gives you twenty-five organizations that are each a weak match for something. Four separate lists give you a hundred organizations that are each a strong match for one thing.

Do this — four separate sheets
  • Help paying rent and utilities in Toledo, OH
  • Car repair assistance in Toledo, OH
  • Medical bill assistance in Toledo, OH
  • Startup funding for a home bakery in Toledo, OH
Not this — one combined sheet
  • "Rent, my car, my medical bills and my business idea in Toledo, OH"

You will get a shallow, generic list — and you will not know which number to call about what.

More than one problem? Good.

Make more than one call sheet. There is no limit, and each one takes minutes to request. Members with four sheets find four times as much.

03 · Getting clear Vague in, vague out
Before you ask

Know exactly what you're asking for.

This is the step members skip, and it is the step that decides whether your call sheet is worth anything. A vague request produces a vague list.

Answer these seven questions for yourself before you join a class, open the AI Researcher, or post in the Questions Channel.

The seven questions
  • Which city and state do you need help in?
  • What is your ZIP code?
  • What is the one problem this sheet is for?
  • If someone handed you the money tomorrow, what exactly would it pay for?
  • Who is it for — you, your household, your business?
  • Which of these describe you: veteran, senior, disabled, single parent, low income, student, caregiver, rural?
  • Roughly how much money would solve it?
Still not sure? Try these three
  • What bill is keeping you up at night? That is your first sheet.
  • What would change your life in six months? A working car, a certification, a business licence. That is your second sheet.
  • What do you keep saying no to because you can't afford it? Dental work, a repair, a move. That is your third sheet.

Bring your answer to a class exactly as you wrote it. Our grant coaches would far rather sharpen a specific request than guess at a vague one.

    05 · Three ways Try all three
    Your options

    Three ways. Use all of them.

    These are not alternatives to choose between. Each one finds organizations the others miss, and members who use all three end up with the deepest lists.

    01

    Join a Call Sheet Class

    A live class where the team explains what a call sheet is, how it is built, and how to use it. If there is time afterwards, team members build sheets for members live — they will take down your problem, your ZIP code and your state, so have all three ready.

    Best when you are new, or your situation is complicated and you want a person to look at it.

    02

    Use the AI Researcher app

    Our own AI tool, built for this community and loaded with Matthew Lesko's research. Enter your ZIP code and your problem, and it builds your list as a spreadsheet you can download.

    Best when you want a list right now, at 2am, without waiting — or you want several lists for several problems.

    03

    Ask in the Questions Channel

    Post your request and a team member builds a call sheet for you, usually as a shareable spreadsheet with notes on your situation. Ask as many questions as you like — one post per problem.

    Best when you want a human-checked list and can wait for a reply. Also the right place for follow-ups.

    Do all three, in this order
    1. Run the AI Researcher first. It costs minutes and gives you a list to react to — far easier than starting from a blank page.
    2. Bring that list to a class. Now you have something specific to ask: "number 14 has no phone number", "I don't understand number 8". Fastest way to learn.
    3. Post the leftovers in the Questions Channel. Anything still unclear, or anything the AI could not find.
    Nervous about showing up? Come anyway.

    Everyone in that room came because they needed something. Nobody is judging you. And if you would rather not be recognised — use an avatar picture and a different display name, keep your camera off, type in the chat instead of speaking. You get exactly the same help.

    The only thing that stops you getting a call sheet is not asking for one.

    06 · The columns What "complete" looks like
    Quality check

    Fourteen columns. Nothing missing.

    This is the column set our team uses. If your sheet is missing these, it is not finished — ask for the missing pieces.

    #ColumnWhy you need it
    01OrganizationWho you are calling. Say it back when they answer.
    02WebsiteFull link. Your first stop for checking the sheet is right.
    03EmailYour backup when nobody picks up.
    04PhoneWith the direct extension — it skips the switchboard.
    05Contact personA name to ask for gets you past the front desk.
    06EligibilityWhether you qualify. Read before dialling.
    07Area servedWhich counties or ZIPs. This is why your ZIP matters.
    08DatesApplication windows. Some open once a year.
    09ServicesWhat they actually do, in plain words.
    10Value of free helpCounselling, training and legal aid are worth real money.
    11Grant or loanMoney you keep versus money you repay.
    12Maximum amountLets you sort and call the biggest ones first.
    13Things to considerThe catches — restrictions, waiting lists, documents.
    1430-second scriptExactly what to say. Removes the hardest part of the call.
    Two habits that separate good sheets from great ones
    • Ask for 25 organizations, not 10. Most calls end in "no" or "not right now". Twenty-five is roughly what it takes to find the two or three that say yes.
    • Ask for a mix of sources. Government, non-profit, private foundations, corporate giving and niche organizations. A sheet that is all government agencies is missing half the money.

    07 · Verify Ten minutes well spent
    Before you dial

    Check the sheet. AI can make mistakes.

    AI tools are excellent at finding organizations you would never have heard of. They are much less reliable at getting the contact details exactly right.

    Phone numbers are where AI slips most

    Independent testing has found that roughly a third of phone numbers produced by AI assistants do not match the organization's own published number. That does not make the tool useless — it makes checking part of the job. A wrong number is a two-minute fix, not a reason to throw the list away.

    Your ten-minute check
    • Open the website first. Dead link or unrelated page? Treat the whole row as unverified.
    • Compare the phone number to the one on the organization's own contact page. If they differ, the website wins.
    • Check the area against your ZIP code. Programs that don't serve your county cannot help you.
    • Check the dates. A closed window goes to the bottom, with a note to call back.
    • Flag what you couldn't confirm. Don't delete it — bring it to a class.
    Found something wrong? Bring it to a class.

    If a name looks off, a number doesn't work, or a listing makes no sense, do not just cross it out. Join a Call Sheet Class, an AI Workshop or a Q&A and ask a grant coach. Nine times out of ten there is a real organization behind a garbled listing, and a coach finds the correct details in seconds. That is exactly what the classes are for.

    08 · Making the calls Where the money happens
    Working it

    A sheet in your downloads has never paid anyone's rent.

    Before you dial
    • Gather your paperwork. The bills, proof of income, proof of address, ID. Having them to hand turns a callback into a same-call decision.
    • Read the script in column 14 out loud once. The first call is the hardest. The tenth is easy.
    • Block out time. Most offices keep business hours and close for lunch. Two hours on a Tuesday morning beats ten minutes here and there.
    Ask these four on every call
    1. "Do I qualify, based on where I live and my situation?" Get a yes or no first.
    2. "What exactly do I send you, and by when?" Write down every document and deadline.
    3. "Is there anyone else you'd recommend I call?" The highest-value question on the sheet. These people know every other program in the county — one call routinely produces three more names.
    4. "Can I have your name and a direct number?" Makes every follow-up ten times easier.
    Write down every outcome

    Add these columns to your sheet as you go. This record becomes as valuable as the original list.

    DateWhoOutcomeNext stepFollow up
    Aug 12Denise, x214Qualify — app sentReturn form + 2 billsAug 15
    Aug 12VoicemailNo answerCall back AMAug 13
    Aug 12Marcus, intakeWrong countyGave 2 other numbersAdded to sheet
    Expect "no" — it is the process, not a verdict on you

    Funds run out, windows close, criteria shift. A "no" from one organization tells you nothing about the next. A program with no money in August may have a new budget in October. Ask when to call back, note it, move to the next line.

    09 · Repeat The part that works
    Keep going

    Do it again. And again.

    One call sheet is a start. It is not the method. The members who get funded are the ones who repeat this loop over and over.

    Five ways to repeat
    • Across your problems. A separate sheet for every distinct need. Four problems, four sheets.
    • With different wording. Run the AI Researcher again for "emergency financial assistance", then "hardship funds", then "utility assistance programs". Different words surface different organizations — and it costs you nothing.
    • Across the classes. Attend more than one. Every coach has a different angle, and other members' questions find money you didn't know to look for.
    • Your calls. Every "not right now" goes on a callback list with a date. New budget cycles open constantly.
    • Every few months. Programs launch, close and change all year. A sheet built in spring is stale by autumn.
    There is no limit

    No cap on how many call sheets you request, how many classes you attend, or how many questions you post. Use all of it.

    10 · Safety & privacy Precise, not identifiable
    Protect yourself

    Be precise. Stay private.

    Never post these

    Classes and the Questions Channel are visible to other members. Assume a stranger will read whatever you post.

    • Phone number · email address · street address
    • Social Security number · date of birth
    • Bank account or card details
    • Photos of any document or ID
    • Case, claim or benefit numbers
    Safe to share

    Your city, your state, your ZIP code, and a description of your problem. That is genuinely all anyone needs to build you a good call sheet.

    "A disabled veteran in ZIP 43604 needing roughly $2,400 for back rent" is precise, useful, and identifies nobody. Every organization will ask for the private details later — on the phone, where they belong.

    Use an avatar if you'd rather not be recognised

    Set a picture that isn't your face and a display name that isn't your legal name. Keep your camera off. None of this reduces the support you get, and nobody will ask why.

    Real program, or scam?

    Everything on a proper call sheet is an organization you contact. That is the safe direction. Be suspicious of anything that contacts you first:

    • Anyone charging a fee to apply. Government grant applications are free.
    • Anyone saying you won a grant you never applied for.
    • Anyone wanting payment by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency.
    • Anyone calling out of the blue for your Social Security number or bank details.

    If it feels wrong, stop and bring it to a class. Report grant scams to the Federal Trade Commission at 1-877-382-4357.

    11 · Bookmarks & last word Don't lose your answers
    Keeping track

    Bookmark every question you ask.

    Replies can arrive days later and the channel moves fast. Without a bookmark you will lose track of where your answer went.

    Your bookmarks live under your personal settings, in Bookmarked Posts — so your call sheets, the coach's notes and your follow-ups all stay in one place.

    • Bookmark your post the moment you publish it, before you close the tab.
    • Bookmark the reply containing your finished call sheet — that is the link you'll keep returning to.
    • Bookmark other members' questions when the answer helps you. Somebody has usually already asked yours.

    There is fuller guidance on writing a good question pinned inside the Questions Channel. Read it once before your first post.

    A note about AI, and about asking for help

    AI can make mistakes. The AI Researcher and every other AI tool can misspell an organization's name, give a number that rings nowhere, list a program that closed last year, or state an eligibility rule slightly wrong. Always check a listing against the organization's own website before relying on it.

    If anything is unclear or looks wrong, come and ask. Join a Call Sheet Class, an AI Workshop, a Q&A or a drop-in clinic and put it in front of a grant coach. Bring the sheet, point at the row, ask. Getting the correct details lined up is exactly what our coaches are here for, and there is no such thing as a question that is too small.

    Nothing in this guide is legal or financial advice, and no organization on any call sheet is guaranteed to have funds available for you.

    Now go build your first sheet. ♥
